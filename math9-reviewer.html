Perpendicular & Parallel Lines

Term 1 · Week 2

Perpendicular Lines

Two lines that intersect to form a perfect 90° angle — a right angle.

Real-world: window grilles, floor tiles, the corner of a picture frame.

Parallel Lines

Straight lines in a plane that are always the same distance apart (equidistant) and never intersect, however far extended.

Real-world: road lane markings, the strings of a guitar, railway tracks.

Construction: Perpendicular to a Line from a Point on the Line

ℓ is drawn as the same full two-way ray in every panel, and D / T / K / S keep the same coordinates throughout — each step just adds one new mark, so you can watch the construction build instead of re-reading a new picture each time.

Construction: Parallel Line Through a Point

ℓ, t and m are drawn as full two-way rays from the moment they appear, and P and the transversal stay in the exact same spot in every panel, so ∠2 and line m end up exactly where the construction says they should.

Example 1. ∠1 = 71°. Find the rest, given a∥b cut by transversal c.

∠2 = 180° − 71° = 109° (linear pair with ∠1)
∠3 = 71° (vertical to ∠1) · ∠4 = 109° (vertical to ∠2)
∠5 = 109° · ∠6 = 71° · ∠7 = 71° · ∠8 = 109° (corresponding to 1‑4)

Example 2. ∠(4x+5)° and ∠(5x−20)° are corresponding angles; a 105° angle is a linear pair with (4x+5)°. Find x.

Corresponding angles are congruent: 4x+5 = 5x−20 → 5 = x−20 → x = 25
(4x+5)° = 4(25)+5 = 105°, (5x−20)° = 5(25)−20 = 105°
Linear pair with 105° → ∠1 = 180−105 = 75°

Relations & Functions

Expressing relationships

Relation

A connection between two sets of values, represented with ordered pairs.

{(1,2), (2,4), (3,6), (4,8)}input (x) → output (y)

Function

A special relation where each input has exactly one output.

ONE INPUT → EXACTLY ONE OUTPUT

5 Ways to Represent a Function

A. Ordered Pairs

(x, y) — e.g. {(1,2),(2,4),(3,6)}

B. Table of Values

x: 1 2 3 4
y: 2 4 6 8

C. Mapping Diagram

D. Graph

Plot ordered pairs on the coordinate plane.

E. Equation

y = 2x → y is twice x

Domain & Range

Domain = input/x-values.
Range = output/y-values.
{(1,2),(2,4),(3,6),(4,8)} → D:{1,2,3,4} R:{2,4,6,8}

Identifying Functions

Rule: if one input has more than one output → NOT a function.

Vertical Line Test

If a vertical line crosses a graph at only one point → function. Two or more points → not a function.

Types of Functions

Linear Function

y = mx + b

Graph is a straight line. m = slope, b = y‑intercept.

Rational Function

y = 1/(x − 2)

Ratio of two polynomials. Denominator ≠ 0 (here x ≠ 2 is excluded from the domain).

Quadratic Function

y = ax² + bx + c, a ≠ 0

Graph is a U‑shaped parabola.

Absolute Value Function

y = |x|

Always ≥ 0 — distance from zero. Graph is V‑shaped.

Word‑problem pattern: ₱20 per notebook → x = number of notebooks (input), y = total cost (output) → y = 20x. If x = 3, y = ₱60.

From Two Points

(2,3) and (5,9): m = (9−3)÷(5−2) = 6÷3 = 2

Always subtract in the same order: (y₂−y₁)÷(x₂−x₁).

From a Graph

Points (1,2) and (3,6): Rise = 6−2 = 4, Run = 3−1 = 2 → m = 4÷2 = 2

Shortcut: up/down = y, right/left = x.

From an Equation

y = mx + b → m is the coefficient of x.

  • y = 3x + 5 → m = 3
  • y = x + 4 → m = 1 (x = 1x)
  • 2x + y = 8 → y = −2x + 8 → m = −2

From a Table

x: 1 2 3 4
y: 3 5 7 9

Δy = +2, Δx = +1 → m = 2 (negative if y decreases as x increases)

Rate of Change

Rate of Change = Slope = Δoutput ÷ Δinput.

Distance: 60km→120km while Time: 1h→2h → (120−60)÷(2−1) = 60 km/hour

Writing the Equation

Slope + y‑intercept

y = mx + b

m=3, b=2 → y = 3x + 2

Slope + One Point

y − y₁ = m(x − x₁)

m=2, (3,7) → y−7=2(x−3) → y = 2x + 1

From a Table

Find m (Δy/Δx), then b (y when x=0).
x:0,1,2,3 / y:4,6,8,10 → m=2, b=4 → y = 2x+4

y = 2x+1 → substitute x‑values:
x=0→1, x=1→3, x=2→5

Conditions Guaranteeing Parallelism & Perpendicularity

A. Parallelism

  • By Definition — coplanar lines that do not intersect.
  • Three Parallel Lines Theorem — if two coplanar lines are each parallel to a third line, they're parallel to each other.
  • Symbol — a ∥ b
  • Equal Slopes — two lines with the same slope are parallel.
  • Converse of Transversal Angles Theorem — if corresponding angles are congruent, the lines are parallel.
  • Parallel Postulate — through a point not on a line, exactly one parallel line can be drawn.

B. Perpendicularity

  • By Definition — lines that intersect to form a right angle.
  • Symbol — a ⟂ b
  • Negative Slope Reciprocal — two non‑vertical lines are ⟂ iff their slopes are negative reciprocals (e.g. m=−⅓ and m=3).
  • Congruent Adjacent Angles — if intersecting lines form congruent adjacent angles, they're perpendicular.
  • Perpendicular Bisector — a line/ray/segment through a segment's midpoint, perpendicular to it.
  • Perpendicular Transversal of Parallel Lines — if a transversal is ⟂ to one of two parallel lines, it's ⟂ to the other.

Negative reciprocals: (−⅓) × 3 = −1 → the lines are perpendicular.

Classifying Quadrilaterals

A quadrilateral is a polygon with 4 sides, 4 vertices, 4 angles — interior angles always sum to 360°.

Parallelogram

2 pairs of parallel opposite sides. Opposite sides & angles congruent; consecutive angles supplementary; diagonals bisect each other.

Rectangle

Parallelogram + 4 right angles. Diagonals congruent & bisect each other.

Rhombus

Parallelogram + 4 congruent sides. Diagonals perpendicular & bisect each other.

Square

4 congruent sides + 4 right angles. Diagonals congruent, perpendicular, bisect each other. Both a rectangle & a rhombus.

Kite

2 distinct pairs of adjacent congruent sides. Diagonals perpendicular; one bisects the other.

Trapezoid

At least 1 pair of parallel sides.

Worked Problems

Parallelogram — top side = 3x+4 cm, opposite bottom side = 5x−10 cm. Find x and each side length.

Property: opposite sides of a parallelogram are congruent.
3x+4 = 5x−10 → −2x = −14 → x = 7
Side AB = 3(7)+4 = 25 cm · Side DC = 5(7)−10 = 25 cm

Parallelogram — two consecutive angles are (3x+15)° and (2x+10)°. Find x and each angle.

Property: consecutive angles are supplementary.
(3x+15)+(2x+10)=180 → 5x+25=180 → x = 31
Angle 1 = 3(31)+15 = 108° · Angle 2 = 2(31)+10 = 72°

Rhombus — sides are (2x+4) cm and (3x−6) cm. Find x and the side length.

Property: all 4 sides of a rhombus are congruent.
2x+4 = 3x−6 → x = 10
Side = 2(10)+4 = 24 cm

Rhombus — two sides measure (3x+4) cm and (5x−8) cm. Find x and the side length.

3x+4 = 5x−8 → 12 = 2x → x = 6
Side = 3(6)+4 = 22 cm

Rectangle — one angle measures (4x+10)°. Find x and all four angles.

Property: a rectangle has four right angles.
4x+10 = 90 → x = 20
All four angles = 90°

Rectangle — diagonals measure (3x+5) m and (5x−11) m. Find x and each diagonal.

Property: diagonals of a rectangle are congruent.
3x+5 = 5x−11 → 16 = 2x → x = 8
Diagonal = 3(8)+5 = 29 m

Square — diagonals measure (3x+5) cm and (5x−11) cm. Find x and each diagonal.

Same as a rectangle's diagonal property → x = 8, each diagonal = 29 cm

Square — a 90° corner has a diagonal drawn to the opposite corner, splitting it into two equal angles. Find each angle.

Property: diagonals of a square bisect opposite angles.
90° ÷ 2 = 45° and 45°

Proving Properties of Parallelograms

Term 1 · Week 10

What a Proof Needs

Given

Information already provided.

Prove

What you need to show.

Statements & Reasons

Facts written one step at a time, each with why it's true.

Proof Toolbox

#1 — Parallel Lines

  • Alternate interior angles are congruent.
  • Same‑side interior angles are supplementary.

#2 — Congruent Triangles

SSS · SAS · ASA · AAS — if two triangles are congruent, corresponding parts are congruent (CPCTC).

#3 — Other Facts

  • Reflexive Property: a segment is congruent to itself (AC ≅ AC).
  • Vertical Angles Theorem: vertical angles are congruent.

Parallelogram Theorems (used in proofs)

  1. A diagonal of a parallelogram divides it into two congruent triangles.
  2. Opposite sides of a parallelogram are congruent.
  3. Consecutive angles are supplementary.
  4. Opposite angles are congruent.
  5. The diagonals of a parallelogram bisect each other.

Two‑Column Proof

Organizes reasoning into two columns:

StatementsReasons
What you know or concludeWhy the statement is true

Used when several statements each need their own separate reason.

Not All Proofs Look the Same

When a result follows directly from an already‑proven theorem, or the reasoning is mainly angle relationships or algebra, a shorter logical proof (Given → Logical Steps → Conclusion) can be clearer than a two‑column layout.
